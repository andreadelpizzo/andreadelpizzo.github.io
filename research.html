Working Papers

  • Indirect Estimators of Intergenerational Mobility
    with Martin Nybom and Jan Stuhler
    › Abstract › Draft

    This chapter reviews indirect estimators of intergenerational mobility, focusing on approaches that infer parent–child or other family associations when direct income data are incomplete or unavailable. We synthesize methods based on instrumental variables, imputation using observable characteristics such as education and occupation, surname-based estimators, and multigenerational linkages. To unify these approaches, we introduce a stylized framework in which socioeconomic status is transmitted through multiple pathways with heterogeneous persistence rates. Within this framework, both direct and indirect estimators can be interpreted as weighted averages of these underlying transmission channels. A central insight is that the choice of instrument or imputation strategy determines these weights, leading different methods to capture distinct aspects of the transmission process. We highlight implications for interpretation, showing that indirect estimators need not recover conventional parent–child correlations but can instead provide complementary evidence on long-run persistence and the mechanisms underlying persistent inequalities.

  • Latent Stratification and the Kinship Network
    › Abstract

    This paper shows that similarities across distant relatives reflect underlying ``social stratification''. When persistent latent advantages also determine who marries whom, they spread across families rather than remaining confined to isolated dynasties. Families with similar advantages therefore become connected to one another, so that these advantages can be interpreted as a form of social position. Using Swedish population registers, I exploit information from distant relatives across multiple socioeconomic outcomes to recover a discrete empirical approximation of this latent social position. The resulting groups display the key features of social stratification: family and marriage ties occur disproportionately within groups, and group differences persist across generations and socioeconomic outcomes. Yet they explain only a limited share of variation in any single outcome. This suggests that substantial economic mobility can coexist with persistent social stratification.

  • What's in a Surname? Multiplicity in Intergenerational Transmission
    with Jan Stuhler
    › Abstract

    The interpretation of surname-based estimators of intergenerational persistence remains contested. We show that surname-based estimators identify long-run persistence through two mechanisms: they reduce attenuation by averaging across individuals, and they systematically place larger weights on traits that persist across generations. Similarities within surname groups reflect common ancestry, so only traits that persist across multiple generations remain shared across surname members. Using linked U.S. Census data from 1920-1940, we show that surname-based estimators exceed standard parent-child estimates even after addressing the main sources of attenuation bias. We further show that variation across surnames is driven primarily by persistent characteristics, which in our setting are mainly observable traits such as geography and ethnicity. These findings support the view that heterogeneity in trait-specific persistence is central to understanding long-run intergenerational persistence.
